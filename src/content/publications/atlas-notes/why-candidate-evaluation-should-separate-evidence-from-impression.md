Interviews create impressions quickly.

A candidate appears confident. Another communicates clearly. Someone seems commercially strong, technically credible or naturally suited to the organization.

These impressions matter. Hiring is a human decision, and judgement cannot — and should not — be removed from it.

But an impression is not the same as evidence.

The challenge in candidate evaluation is therefore not to eliminate judgement. It is to make clear what that judgement is based on.

## Interviews naturally create impressions

Every interview produces two things at the same time.

The first is information: what the candidate has done, the environments they have worked in, the decisions they have made and the results they can explain.

The second is impression: how confident, credible, articulate, experienced or compatible the candidate appears.

The two are easy to combine.

A candidate who communicates exceptionally well may quickly be perceived as commercially strong. Someone with an impressive employer on their CV may be assumed to have operated at a high level. A candidate with experience in the same industry may feel safer than someone coming from an adjacent market.

None of those impressions is necessarily wrong.

The problem begins when we stop asking what evidence supports them.

> “The purpose of structured evaluation is not to eliminate judgement. It is to make clear what that judgement is based on.”

## An evaluation statement should be traceable to evidence

Consider two observations:

*“Strong commercial profile.”*

and:

*“Built a new territory from zero, developed strategic accounts and can clearly explain the sales process, stakeholders and obstacles involved.”*

The first is an impression.

The second gives us evidence that helps explain why the impression exists.

The same applies to leadership.

*“Good leadership presence.”*

tells us very little about what the candidate has actually demonstrated.

A more useful observation might be:

*“Led a 14-person service organization, reorganized field responsibilities and can explain how performance issues within the team were addressed.”*

This does not automatically prove that the person will be an effective leader in the next organization.

But it gives the hiring team something concrete to evaluate, question and compare.

That distinction matters because hiring decisions should not depend only on whether an interviewer feels confident about a candidate.

They should also make visible why that confidence exists.

## Confidence and evidence are different things

Hiring teams often become increasingly confident as a process progresses.

That confidence can come from strong evidence.

But it can also come from familiarity.

A candidate may have completed several interviews. Multiple stakeholders may like the person. Conversations become easier. The candidate begins to feel familiar to the organization.

Confidence increases.

The amount or quality of evidence, however, may not have increased at the same rate.

The opposite can also happen.

A candidate may initially create a weaker impression but provide increasingly strong evidence when questioned in depth about previous decisions, technical challenges, leadership situations or commercial achievements.

This is why confidence and evidence should be treated as related, but separate, concepts.

A useful question late in a hiring process is not only:

*“How confident are we in this candidate?”*

It is also:

*“What is that confidence based on?”*

## Not all evidence is equally strong

A CV contains evidence.

An interview contains evidence.

References can provide evidence.

A practical case can provide evidence.

But they do not necessarily answer the same questions or provide the same level of confidence.

Imagine a candidate being evaluated for a leadership role requiring commercial development.

Their CV states that they were responsible for business development.

That is one piece of evidence.

During the interview, they explain which customers they personally developed, how opportunities were identified, who participated in the sales process and which decisions they owned.

That adds another layer.

A practical discussion might then test how they would approach a comparable commercial challenge in the new organization.

A reference might provide another perspective on how independently they actually operated.

Each source contributes differently.

The objective is not to accumulate as much information as possible.

It is to understand whether the evidence available is sufficiently relevant and sufficiently strong for the decision being made.

## Structure makes disagreement more useful

Different interviewers will not always reach the same conclusion about a candidate.

That is normal.

One stakeholder may be impressed by technical depth. Another may question leadership maturity. Someone else may see strong commercial potential.

Trying to eliminate those differences entirely is neither realistic nor necessarily desirable.

The more important question is whether the disagreement can be explained.

Consider:

**Interviewer A:** *“I think she is a strong leader.”*

**Interviewer B:** *“I am not convinced.”*

That discussion can easily become a comparison of impressions.

But if the evaluation is structured around evidence, the conversation changes:

What leadership situations did she actually describe?

How large was the team?

What decisions did she own?

How did she handle underperformance?

Has she led through change?

What evidence supports the concern?

What evidence is still missing?

Now disagreement becomes useful.

Instead of forcing consensus too early, the hiring team can identify exactly where interpretations differ and where additional evidence may be needed.

> “Structured evaluation does not require everyone to have the same opinion. It makes the reasons behind different opinions visible.”

## Separate dimensions before forming the overall judgement

Another common problem occurs when several candidate characteristics collapse into one overall impression.

A candidate may be described simply as:

*“Strong candidate.”*

But strong in what way?

For an industrial leadership search, the hiring decision may involve several distinct dimensions:

**Technical & Functional Capability**
Does the candidate have the knowledge and experience required to understand the function and its operational realities?

**Business & Leadership Judgement**
Can the candidate make decisions, lead others, establish priorities and operate at the required level of responsibility?

**Organizational & Contextual Fit**
Can the candidate operate effectively within this particular organization, leadership structure, market and international context?

**Motivation & Trajectory**
Does the opportunity make sense given the candidate's motivations, career direction and expectations?

A candidate can be strong in one dimension and uncertain in another.

That information is more useful than reducing the entire evaluation to a single score or overall impression.

It also makes trade-offs visible.

The technically strongest candidate may not have the strongest leadership evidence.

The candidate with the closest industry background may not demonstrate the strongest business judgement.

The most polished interviewer may not have the strongest evidence of execution.

Separating dimensions allows the hiring team to see those differences before deciding how much they matter.

## Structure does not require scoring

Structured evaluation is sometimes associated with numerical scorecards.

That is one possible approach, but structure does not require turning every observation into a number.

A score such as 8/10 for leadership can appear precise while hiding the reasoning behind it.

Why eight?

What evidence would have produced a six?

What would a ten look like?

And did two interviewers use the scale in the same way?

Numbers can be useful when the underlying criteria and evidence are clear. Without that foundation, they can create an impression of objectivity without necessarily improving the decision.

A structured evaluation can instead document:

- what the role requires,
- what evidence was observed,
- where that evidence came from,
- what remains uncertain,
- and where different interpretations exist.

The objective is not mathematical precision.

It is decision clarity.

## Good evaluation also records what is missing

One of the most useful outcomes of candidate evaluation is identifying uncertainty.

Hiring teams naturally focus on the evidence they have.

But the absence of evidence can be equally important.

Perhaps the candidate has demonstrated strong technical expertise but has never led a team of comparable size.

Perhaps the candidate has successfully managed existing customers but there is limited evidence of building new business.

Perhaps the candidate has worked in international organizations but has never reported directly across cultures or time zones.

These gaps do not automatically disqualify someone.

They identify what the hiring team still needs to understand.

This creates a much more useful question than:

*“Do we like this candidate?”*

The question becomes:

*“What do we know, what do we believe, and what do we still need to validate?”*

## Better evidence does not remove uncertainty

No hiring process can prove exactly how someone will perform in a future organization.

The environment will be different.

The team will be different.

The manager will be different.

The market may be different.

And people themselves continue to develop.

Structured evaluation therefore should not create the illusion that uncertainty can be eliminated.

Its purpose is to make uncertainty more visible.

A strong hiring decision is not one in which every question has disappeared.

It is one in which the organization understands the evidence supporting the decision, recognizes where uncertainty remains and consciously decides whether that uncertainty is acceptable. ## Human judgement remains essential

There is a risk in discussing structured evaluation that the process begins to sound mechanical.

It should not be.

Hiring involves context, interpretation and judgement.

A candidate's previous experience needs to be understood relative to the environment in which it occurred. Transferability must be considered. Motivation needs interpretation. Different pieces of evidence sometimes point in different directions.

Human judgement is necessary to make sense of that complexity.

The role of structure is not to replace that judgement.

It is to improve the information available to it.

The strongest evaluation process therefore combines both:

structured evidence and human interpretation.

Neither is sufficient on its own.

## A better question for the next candidate interview

Instead of asking only:

*“What did we think of the candidate?”*

ask:

*“What evidence changed our understanding of the candidate?”*

That small distinction can change the quality of the discussion that follows.
