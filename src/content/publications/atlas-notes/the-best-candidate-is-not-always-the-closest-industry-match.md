When an industrial company begins a search, one requirement often appears almost automatically: experience in the same industry.

For a machinery manufacturer, that may mean candidates from machinery manufacturers. For an automation business, candidates from automation competitors. For a packaging company, people who have already worked in packaging.

The reasoning is understandable. Industry familiarity can shorten the learning curve, provide access to relevant customers and reduce uncertainty about the candidate's ability to operate in a specialized environment.

But it can also create a hidden assumption: that the candidate whose previous employer looks most similar is necessarily the candidate best equipped to succeed.

That assumption deserves examination.

A hiring decision is not a comparison of company names. It is a judgement about the capabilities required to deliver results in a specific organizational context.

> “The closest industry match is not necessarily the strongest capability match.”

## Why industry experience becomes the default

Industry experience is an efficient screening criterion. It is visible on a CV, easy to explain internally and appears to reduce the risk of hiring someone unfamiliar with the product, customers or operating environment.

In specialized industrial markets, that logic is not unreasonable. Understanding capital equipment sales cycles, manufacturing processes, technical specifications or the service expectations of industrial customers can matter substantially.

The problem begins when industry experience moves from being one relevant source of evidence to becoming an unquestioned proxy for all the capabilities a role requires.

A candidate may know the industry extremely well but have limited experience developing new markets, leading a team or building a commercial organization.

Another candidate may come from an adjacent sector but have demonstrated those capabilities repeatedly.

The title and industry label alone cannot resolve the comparison.

## When direct industry experience really matters

Not every requirement is transferable.

Direct experience may be particularly important when the role depends on:

- highly specialized product or process knowledge that requires years to acquire;
- regulatory, safety or compliance requirements that cannot be learned quickly;
- established relationships with a narrow customer ecosystem;
- technical credibility in conversations with demanding specialists;
- a short time-to-impact in an environment with limited onboarding capacity.

For example, a senior technical specialist expected to independently diagnose highly specific equipment failures may need direct product or process experience from day one.

A company should not relax that requirement merely to enlarge the candidate pool.

The correct question is not whether industry experience matters. It is which elements of that experience are essential, how difficult they are to acquire, and how directly they contribute to the mandate.

## What can transfer across industries

Other capabilities may travel across adjacent industrial environments.

Examples include:

- consultative technical selling and complex B2B sales cycles;
- developing new territories or customer segments;
- managing distributors and strategic accounts;
- leading field-service organizations;
- building processes and teams in growing subsidiaries;
- working with multinational headquarters;
- managing complex projects involving engineering, operations and customers.

A commercial leader who has successfully sold industrial automation solutions may possess capabilities relevant to a packaging machinery manufacturer, even without direct packaging experience.

Likewise, a service leader from one machinery segment may understand installed-base management, field-team coordination, customer escalation and service profitability in ways that transfer to another equipment category.

Transferability should not be assumed. It should be investigated through evidence.

## An illustrative industrial hiring decision

*Illustrative / fictional scenario. This is not a real Sync Talent engagement, client case or identifiable candidate.*

Consider a fictional international packaging automation company hiring a Regional Sales Manager in Mexico.

The mandate is to expand the customer base, develop new opportunities, manage technically complex sales cycles and collaborate with international headquarters.

Two candidates reach the final stage.

**Candidate A — Direct industry match.** Has several years of packaging machinery experience, understands the products and customer ecosystem, and brings established industry relationships.

However, the available evidence of independently building a new territory is limited. Most previous commercial responsibilities involved established accounts.

**Candidate B — Adjacent industry match.** Comes from industrial automation, has sold complex technical solutions, built a territory from an early stage and managed long sales cycles with industrial customers.

However, packaging-specific knowledge and direct relationships in that segment remain less developed.

Neither profile is automatically stronger.

If immediate product expertise and established customer access are essential, Candidate A may be more suitable.

If independent market development and complex technical selling are the critical success factors, Candidate B may warrant greater consideration.

The decision depends on the actual mandate, the quality of the evidence and the organization's capacity to support the learning curve.

## Separate requirements from preferences

One useful discipline is to distinguish three kinds of requirements.

**Non-negotiable.** Capabilities or knowledge the person must already possess to perform the role safely and effectively.

**Transferable.** Capabilities that can be demonstrated in another industry or organizational context.

**Learnable.** Product, process or market knowledge that the organization can realistically develop after hiring.

The classification is specific to the mandate. A capability considered learnable in one company may be non-negotiable in another because of customer expectations, onboarding resources or time pressure.

This distinction should happen before sourcing begins, not after a preferred candidate appears.

## Evaluate transferability through evidence

An adjacent-industry candidate should not be accepted simply because the interviewer believes the person is adaptable.

Transferability requires evidence.

Ask:

- What comparable problems has the candidate solved?
- How similar were the customer, product and decision-making environments?
- Which capabilities were demonstrated personally?
- What changed because of the candidate's actions?
- Which elements of the new role would require meaningful learning?
- How realistic is that learning curve?

For a commercial role, relevant evidence may include how the candidate developed accounts, navigated complex buying committees, collaborated with engineers and managed long sales cycles.

For a service leadership role, it may include field-team management, customer escalation, installed-base economics and cross-border coordination.

This is also where structured evaluation becomes useful. Instead of treating an industry label as a verdict, the hiring team can compare what is known, what is inferred and what remains uncertain.

> “Transferability is not a personality trait to assume. It is a capability to investigate.”

## Industry fit is not the same as organizational fit

Even direct industry experience does not guarantee success in a different company.

A candidate from a large multinational with mature processes may struggle in a small Mexican subsidiary where the role requires hands-on execution and ambiguity tolerance.

A successful individual contributor may not be prepared to build a team.

A leader accustomed to an established customer base may not be the right person to open a new market.

Conversely, an adjacent-industry candidate may have worked in an organizational environment much closer to the one being hired into.

The relevant comparison therefore includes not only industry similarity but also the operating context, leadership expectations, business maturity and trajectory of the role.

## Broadening the market without lowering the standard

Expanding a search into adjacent industries should not mean weakening the hiring criteria.

It means identifying which criteria are essential to success and testing whether candidates from other environments can demonstrate them.

This can improve the search in several ways:

- access to candidates who would be excluded by a narrow industry filter;
- a clearer distinction between technical knowledge and broader capability;
- better visibility into trade-offs;
- a more realistic assessment of the available talent market;
- a stronger basis for discussing compensation, onboarding and risk.

The objective is not to prefer adjacent-industry candidates.

It is to avoid excluding potentially strong candidates before evaluating the evidence that matters.

## Hire for the mandate, not for the familiarity of the CV

Industry experience can be valuable. Sometimes it is essential.

But when companies mistake familiarity for capability, they risk narrowing the search before understanding what the role actually requires.

The better question is not simply:

*“Has this person worked in our industry?”*

It is:

*“What evidence shows this person can succeed in the business situation we are hiring for?”*

That is the distinction between screening for similarity and evaluating for success.
